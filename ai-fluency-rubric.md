# AI Fluency Rubric

A plain-English reference framework for measuring how well teams actually use AI—designed to be adapted to a company's specific stack and compliance requirements. Instead of guessing who is "good with AI," it breaks skill down into four clear levels based on what people actually do every day in a regulated financial environment.

---

## Level 1: Beginner (Just Trying It Out)
The beginner does not understand what AI is or how it works, and rarely thinks about it. They see it as a panacea rather than an interface tool. They tend to either trust it unconditionally or not at all.

- **How they prompt:** Types short, vague requests (like *"write an email about our new update"*) and hopes for the best.
- **Quality check:** Copies and pastes the first draft with little editing. Leaves in clichés and robotic phrasing (*"delve," "fast-paced landscape," "game-changing"*) and rarely double-checks facts.
- **Safety & compliance:** Not totally sure what company or client data is off-limits. Relies on someone else to catch regulatory mistakes.
- **Daily workflow:** Opens a chat window occasionally for one-off tasks, with no real system.

## Level 2: Solid User (Gets Good Work Done)
The solid user has had some positive interactions with the free versions of ChatGPT, Claude, or Grok at home. They understand that more accurate input data = more accurate output. They understand the concept of GIGO (Garbage In / Garbage Out). Their initial prompts are more specific than the Beginner, but leave room for improvement. This user tends to stay in their comfort zone and rarely explores outside of it.

- **How they prompt:** Gives the AI basic context—like the audience or goal—and pushes back when a first draft is obviously off, though prompting is still mostly trial-and-error.
- **Quality check:** Catches obvious AI clichés and edits outputs to sound more human. Double-checks major facts, though subtle errors or drift can still slip through.
- **Safety & compliance:** Follows basic privacy rules and avoids pasting obvious sensitive data (like passwords or client SSNs), but may still need guidance on gray-area compliance rules.
- **Daily workflow:** Uses AI regularly for familiar, low-risk tasks like drafting, summarizing, and brainstorming, sticking to basic chat windows.

## Level 3: Power User (Builds Repeatable Systems)
The Power User has integrated AI into their daily workflow. They have a strong sense of AI’s strengths and limitations, and they understand the value of calibrating the model. They diligently watch for hallucinations and regularly course-correct. The Power User knows when and how to prune their chats and when to switch models. They see AI as the powerful interface that it is, and use it appropriately and well.

- **How they prompt:** Does not type the same instructions over and over. Saves reusable prompt templates and custom instructions for recurring tasks.
- **Quality check:** Uses a consistent checklist to catch subtle errors. Knows when to switch tools, tighten the rules, or close the chat and just write it from scratch.
- **Safety & compliance:** Spots FINRA Rule 2210 red flags—like promises, guarantees, or one-sided claims—before a draft ever leaves their desk.
- **Daily workflow:** Connects AI to real work tools (forms, spreadsheets, no-code automations) to save measurable hours every week. Has built and maintained working automations.

## Level 4: Steward (Elevates the Whole Team)
The Steward is not only a Power User themselves, they go out of their way to improve the habits and best practices of their colleagues. They can tell at a glance when a user is struggling and are compelled to help them refine their processes. They see the big picture and understand that a rising tide lifts all boats.

- **How they prompt:** Builds shared prompt templates and simple internal tools that the whole team can use without needing to be experts. Regularly adds how-tos and best practices guidelines to the knowledge base.
- **Quality check:** Sets the standard for the team. Reviews teammates' AI work, points out where the quality is slipping, and coaches people on how to fix it. Offers constructive criticism and helps teammates sharpen their habits.
- **Safety & compliance:** Builds safety checks into the workflow (like automated pre-screeners and mandatory human review steps) before a tool is ever handed to the team.
- **Daily workflow:** Sits with other departments to find real bottlenecks, builds practical fixes, and shuts down tools that people stop using. Constantly simplifies workflows for themselves and others, anticipating friction and fixing it before bottlenecks arise.

---

## When NOT to Use AI
Being good with AI means knowing when to leave it closed. Do not use AI for:

- **Final compliance approval:** AI can flag obvious red flags, but a licensed human always makes the final call.
- **Unchecked math:** Never trust a chatbot to calculate tax credits, fees, or financial models on its own. Always run numbers through a spreadsheet or verified code.
- **High-stakes human moments:** Tough client conversations, performance feedback, or sensitive negotiations need your actual voice and judgment.
- **Outsourcing critical thinking:** NEVER allow AI to do your thinking for you. Use it to temper your instincts, double-check your work, and walk you through confusing technical processes, but never put it in charge.

---

## How This Guide Gets Used
1. **Find the starting line:** Instead of a quiz, people pick their level and share two real examples of their work (the prompt they used and the final edited result).
2. **Train for the gap:** Beginners and Solid Users get hands-on coaching on better prompting, cutting AI slop, and data safety. Power Users learn how to build repeatable workflows for their teams and are encouraged to regularly add to and update the knowledge base with best practices and how-tos.
3. **Check real results:** Every 90 days, audit actual outcomes—fewer compliance rejections, faster turnaround times, and whether people are still using the tools they built.
4. **Protect the learning curve:** Encourage colleagues to challenge themselves and elevate their user level without the threat of penalization. Allow them time to study, practice, and learn. Adopting a new process is difficult, and leadership should expect and allow for a brief period of perceived reduced productivity while people adjust to new workflows.
